Field guide

How intragovernmental debt works

Three levels, meant to be read in order over time. Level 1 gives you the model; Level 2 the plumbing behind the numbers on this page; Level 3 the way a fiscal analyst reads them. Figures in the text update with the statement.

1. Two kinds of lender

Every Treasury security has an owner. When the owner is outside the federal government (a bank, a pension fund, a foreign central bank, a household, the Federal Reserve) it is debt held by the public. When the owner is another part of the federal government it is an intragovernmental holding. Today that split is public and intragovernmental, for a total of .

The intragovernmental owners are federal trust funds and accounts. The four largest in the latest statement are Social Security's Old-Age and Survivors Insurance fund, the Military Retirement Fund, the Civil Service Retirement and Disability Fund, and the DoD Medicare-Eligible Retiree Health Care Fund. Medicare's Hospital Insurance fund is fifth. The bar chart above lists them.

2. Why a trust fund owns Treasuries at all

Take Social Security. Payroll taxes come in every day. Benefits go out every month. By law, income to the trust funds must be invested daily in securities guaranteed as to principal and interest by the federal government (SSA, Trust Fund FAQs); in practice they hold special-issue Treasury securities. So Treasury takes the cash, spends it on whatever the government is spending on, and hands the trust fund a security in return. That security is the IOU. The cash is gone; the claim remains.

A household version: you keep a jar labeled "retirement," but instead of leaving money in it you spend the money on this month's bills and put a signed note in the jar for the same amount. The jar's balance is real to you in the sense that you owe it. It is not real in the sense that nobody outside your house is out any money yet.

3. What each total is good for

  • Debt held by the public is what the government has borrowed from everyone else. It is what the market has to absorb, what drives interest costs that leave the government, and the number economists compare to GDP.
  • Intragovernmental holdings is what one part of the government owes another. It nets out of any consolidated view, but it is a promise: benefits that will have to be paid with real cash later.
  • Total public debt outstanding (gross debt) is the sum. Almost all of it counts against the statutory debt limit ( of in Table II), which is why the ceiling bites on the trust funds too (Level 2).

4. The one-sentence model

Intragovernmental debt is public debt that has been deferred. When a trust fund needs cash it redeems its securities, Treasury raises the cash by borrowing from the public, and the amount moves from the orange band to the blue band on the chart above. The total does not change; who is owed does.

  1. The Federal Reserve holds trillions of dollars of Treasuries. Which band is it in? Public. The Fed is independent of the Treasury; its holdings are debt held by the public even though its profits are remitted to Treasury.
  2. Social Security's two trust funds hold . Is that cash? No. It is Treasury securities. The cash was spent when it came in; the balance is a claim on future general revenue.
  3. If OASI redeems $40B of securities to pay benefits and Treasury borrows $40B from the public to cover it, what happens to total debt? Nothing. Public rises $40B, intragovernmental falls $40B, total is unchanged.

1. The instrument: Government Account Series

Trust funds do not buy the same bills, notes and bonds the market buys. They receive Government Account Series (GAS) securities: nonmarketable and never traded. In Table I, GAS is the line that carries almost all of the intragovernmental column ( of ); the remaining sliver is marketable securities that a few accounts hold directly.

Two flavors exist. Special issues (Social Security, Medicare) carry an interest rate set by formula: the average market yield on marketable Treasuries with four or more years to maturity, rounded to the nearest eighth of a percent, fixed each month for that month's purchases. Market-based GAS (used by many other accounts) mirror the price and yield of a comparable marketable security. Special issues carry no market risk: SSA notes they can be redeemed at any time at face value, a privilege no outside investor has. Market-based GAS are priced like the security they mirror, so their value moves with the market.

2. Issued and redeemed: the daily churn

Table III shows each fund's securities issued and redeemed during the month, and the numbers are enormous relative to the balances. The Military Retirement Fund issued and redeemed in one month against a balance. The gross figures are many times the net change. What matters is the net: issued minus redeemed, which is the fund's cash surplus or deficit for the month. For Social Security's OASI fund it was ; a negative number means the fund redeemed more than it took in that month.

3. Interest is paid in more IOUs

When Treasury owes a trust fund interest, it does not send cash. It credits the fund with more GAS. Social Security's special issues are credited with interest every June 30 and December 31, in new securities (CRS, RS20607). Intragovernmental holdings rose $88.7B in June 2026 and $45.2B in December 2025; the exception was June 2025 (−$5.4B), during the debt-limit standoff. October jumps are larger still (+$222.1B in October 2024, +$109.6B in October 2025). This means intragovernmental holdings can keep growing even while a fund runs a cash deficit, as long as interest credited exceeds the shortfall. It also means the interest never leaves the government: it is an accounting entry, not a payment to anyone outside.

4. Where the debt limit comes in

The statutory limit applies to gross debt, so every dollar a trust fund invests counts against the ceiling. When Treasury runs out of room it uses "extraordinary measures." Treasury's January 2025 list includes suspending or redeeming investments of the Civil Service Retirement fund and the Postal Service Retiree Health Benefits Fund, suspending reinvestment of the TSP G Fund and the Exchange Stabilization Fund, and suspending sales of State and Local Government Series securities. The G Fund sits in the public column, so a standoff shows in both columns. Afterward the CSRDF, PSRHBF and G Fund are made whole; the ESF is not reimbursed for lost interest. In the monthly chart, total debt was flat from January to June 2025, then rose $706B in July.

5. Budget accounting: unified, on-budget, off-budget

Social Security is legally "off-budget," yet the headline deficit everyone quotes is the unified deficit, which includes it. When the trust funds ran surpluses they made the unified deficit look smaller than the rest of the government's borrowing. The Trustees say Social Security's total cost has exceeded its total income every year since 2021 (2025 Trustees summary), so the trust funds' shortfalls now add to what must be borrowed from the public.

6. A wrinkle: GAS held by the public

Table I also shows of Government Account Series in the public column. Most of it is the Thrift Savings Plan's G Fund (). It is issued like intragovernmental debt but the beneficial owners are federal employees, who are members of the public, so it counts as public debt. Suspending its reinvestment is one of Treasury's extraordinary measures in a standoff (Treasury, January 2025).

Check your understanding
  1. A fund shows $100B issued and $95B redeemed. What is its cash position for the month? A $5B surplus. The gross figures are rollover; the net is what the fund actually added to its holdings.
  2. Why do intragovernmental holdings rise in June and December even in a fund that is running a deficit? Interest is credited semiannually, in new securities, on those dates.
  3. Why do debt-limit standoffs show up in the intragovernmental column as well as the public one? The cap binds on the total, so both stall: from December 2024 to June 2025 public debt rose only $117B while intragovernmental holdings fell $124B. The measures themselves work mostly through GAS accounts.
  4. A colleague says "the government can just cancel the debt it owes itself." What is the counterargument? Cancelling the securities does not cancel the benefits they back. The trust fund would run dry sooner, and the general fund would still have to pay the benefits from borrowing or taxes. The IOU is bookkeeping; the obligation is not.

1. The financing identity

The three numbers on this page are linked by an identity worth memorizing:

Δ debt held by the public ≈ unified deficit + other means of financing
Δ intragovernmental ≈ trust-fund net investment (surplus + interest credited − redemptions)
Δ total debt = Δ public + Δ intragovernmental

"Other means of financing" is mostly the change in Treasury's cash balance at the Fed (the TGA) plus direct-loan activity such as student loans. Over the last twelve months the public column grew and the intragovernmental column . Compare the first to the unified deficit from the Monthly Treasury Statement: the gap is the TGA and loan programs. Compare the second to the Trustees' projected trust-fund balances: the gap is the difference between projection and reality.

From the market's point of view this means a given on-budget deficit now needs more marketable issuance than when the trust funds ran surpluses. The 2026 Trustees Report (June 2026) projects OASI depletion in the fourth quarter of 2032; until then its drawdown is financed by borrowing from the public, and after it current law limits benefits to incoming revenue. Medicare's Hospital Insurance fund can pay full benefits through 2033 under the same report.

3. Gross interest vs. net interest

Interest on intragovernmental debt inflates gross interest expense but is excluded from net interest, the figure CBO uses because it is what leaves the government. When you see a claim that interest costs exceed defense or Medicare, check which one is meant. The intragovernmental piece is real to the trust funds (it extends their solvency dates) but is not a cash cost to the Treasury until the securities are redeemed.

4. Which debt-to-GDP ratio

Debt held by the public to GDP is the economically meaningful ratio and the one in CBO's long-term outlook. Gross debt is higher by the intragovernmental holdings, in the latest statement. Neither is wrong, but they answer different questions: how much has the market lent the government, versus how large are the government's total promises, including to itself.

5. The special-issue rate as a policy lever

Social Security's new-issue rate is set each month from yields on marketable Treasuries not due for at least four years, measured on the prior month's last business day (SSA). The fund's average yield is a mix of rates locked in over many years, so it lags the rate cycle; SSA publishes both the new-issue and the effective rate. None of this affects the market directly, but it feeds the solvency projections.

6. Retirement funds are a different kind of entry

The Military Retirement Fund and the Civil Service fund together hold , more than Medicare. Unlike Social Security, which is funded by payroll taxes from outside the government, much of their money comes from other federal accounts. Their balances are largely accrual entries, meant to make each year's budget show the cost of pension promises as they are earned rather than when paid. They are useful for cost transparency; they are not a store of resources.

7. A monthly reading routine

  • OASI net flow in the holders table. Negative is the structural story; a positive month often reflects June or December interest crediting (Level 2).
  • Intragovernmental share in the stat strip. Drifting down is expected; a sharp drop that is not a standoff means a fund is drawing down fast.
  • Public change vs. the unified deficit for the same month (Monthly Treasury Statement, published the 8th business day). A big gap is the TGA being rebuilt or drawn.
  • Bills share of marketable debt. A November 2020 Treasury Borrowing Advisory Committee presentation recommended managing bills to 15 to 20% of marketable debt and put the historical average at 23%. A share above that range means Treasury is leaning on the front end, which rolls over faster.
  • Debt-limit room. Divide it by the trailing monthly change in total debt for a rough count of months to the next standoff.
  • June 30. Social Security's short-term certificates mature on June 30, and any not redeemed are reinvested in longer special-issue bonds (CRS, RS20607), so June's gross issued and redeemed figures are outsized. Read the net.

Check your understanding
  1. The unified deficit was $150B in a month but debt held by the public rose $300B. Where did the other $150B go? Most likely into Treasury's cash balance at the Fed, or into direct loans. It is "other means of financing," not spending.
  2. Trust-fund interest of $70B is credited in June. Does the unified deficit change? No. It is an intragovernmental transfer: an outlay in one account and a receipt in another. Total debt rises, public debt does not.
  3. Why does a falling intragovernmental share matter to a bond investor even though no securities change hands? Because a given on-budget deficit now needs more marketable issuance: trust-fund shortfalls add to borrowing from the public instead of offsetting it.
  4. Two countries both report 120% gross debt to GDP. One has 40 points of it intragovernmental, the other 5. Which has borrowed more from markets? The second. Debt held by the public is 115% vs. 80%.

Reading it. Total public debt outstanding = debt held by the public + intragovernmental holdings. The public share is what has to be financed in the market; the intragovernmental share is an IOU from one part of the government to another and rolls into the public share as trust funds redeem it. The Federal Reserve's holdings count as debt held by the public.
